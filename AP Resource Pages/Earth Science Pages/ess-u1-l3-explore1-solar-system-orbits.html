What are orbits like in our solar system?

The phenomenon

Comet Borrelly has water that is frozen most of the time, but every several years it shoots out a jet of vaporized water and dust.

Your job: examine and graph the Solar System Orbital Data below, looking for evidence of patterns that might help you form a claim about why water exists in different phases on different objects in the solar system — and why the water on Comet Borrelly keeps changing phase.

2 · Build a graph

Pick what goes on each axis, the same choice you would make in Sheets or Excel. Try several combinations. You are looking for relationships that hold for every object — and for objects that break the pattern.

Why the log option is there: some of these numbers are tiny (Mercury, 0.39 AU) and some are huge (Pluto, 39 AU), so the inner planets pile up in one corner. Stretching the axes spreads them out. It does not change the data — only how much of the pattern you can see at once.

3 · How far does each object actually get?

The average distance is only one number. Each bar below runs from an object's minimum distance to its maximum distance from the Sun, with a tick at the average. The shaded bands mark the only distances where this data set actually reports each phase of water.

50 AU

Read this chart carefully

  • For most planets the bar is so short it is almost a dot — their distance from the Sun barely changes.
  • Two bars are long. Find them, and check what the table says about the water on each one.
  • The bands come straight from your data, not from a textbook: vapor is only reported inward of Venus's farthest point, liquid only at Earth's orbit, ice only from Mars's closest point outward. The gaps between bands are distances where this data set simply does not tell you.
