Reaction Time and Stopping Distance

Problem

When something steps into the road you do not brake instantly. For a short reaction time tr you are still travelling at full speed — your foot has not reached the pedal. Only then do the brakes grip, slowing the car at about a = 7.0 m/s² on dry asphalt.

So every stop is two motions in a row: a rectangle of distance while you react, then a triangle while you brake. Drive the three roads you know — the CES school zone at 25 mph, Maple Hill Road at 40 mph and Route 9 at 55 mph — alert (tr = 0.75 s) and reading a text (tr = 2.0 s). Why the two phases behave so differently

The rectangle is the distance you cover while still reacting: d₁ = v tr. Its height is your speed and its width is your reaction time, so it grows in step with v.

The triangle is the braking distance: d₂ = v²/2a. It gets both taller and wider as v rises, so it grows like v².

Picking up a phone changes only the rectangle. The extra distance is Δd = v Δtr — the braking term cancels completely, so your brakes are irrelevant to the cost of the distraction.
