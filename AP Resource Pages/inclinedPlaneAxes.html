Turning the Axes

Why we rotate the coordinate system on an inclined plane — and where sin θ and cos θ actually come from. AP Physics 1 · Unit 2
Drag until the slope lies flat. Watch gravity tilt. It ends up on neither axis — which is exactly why it is the one force you break up.
Fg = mg20.0 N
Fg∥ = mg sin θ10.0 N
Fg⊥ = mg cos θ17.3 N
FN = mg cos θ17.3 N
a = g sin θ5.0 m/s²
g = 10 N/kg · frictionless unless Friction is on

What you are looking at

With the ordinary axes (x across, y up), three of the forces point along awkward directions, and so does the acceleration. You would have to break up FN, the friction, and the acceleration into components. That is three jobs.
With the turned axes (x′ along the slope, y′ perpendicular to it), FN lies entirely on y′, friction lies entirely on x′, and the acceleration lies entirely on x′ — because the block can only slide along the ramp. Only gravity is left to break up. That is one job.

That is the whole reason we rotate. We choose the axes that make the acceleration point along one of them. The CED says so outright — “a coordinate system with one axis parallel to the direction of acceleration simplifies the translation from free-body diagram to algebraic representation” (2.2.B.4).

Where the sine and cosine come from. Turn on The equal angles. The angle at the base of the ramp and the angle between Fg and the perpendicular are the same angle — their arms are mutually perpendicular.

Once you see that, the triangle reads straight off: gravity is the hypotenuse, so the side next to θ (into the slope) is mg cos θ and the side opposite θ (down the slope) is mg sin θ.
Don't memorize which one is which — check it. Slide θ to 0°: the ramp is flat, nothing pulls you along it, and the floor holds your whole weight. So the along-the-slope piece must vanish and the perpendicular piece must be the full mg. Only sin 0 = 0 and cos 0 = 1 do that.

Now slide to 80°, nearly a cliff: almost all of gravity pulls you down the slope and the surface barely presses back at all.

Try these before you leave

  1. Set θ = 45°. What do you notice about Fg∥ and Fg⊥, and why must that be true at exactly 45°?
  2. Turn the board all the way. Which forces now lie exactly along an axis, and which one does not? What does that tell you about which force needs breaking up?
  3. Turn friction on and raise θ slowly. Find the angle where the block would just begin to slip, and say what that tells you about μs.
  4. Without touching the sliders: if the mass doubled, which of the five numbers would change, and which would not?
