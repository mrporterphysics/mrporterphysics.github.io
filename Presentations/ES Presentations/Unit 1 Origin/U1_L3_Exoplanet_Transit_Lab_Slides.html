LAUNCH. Four minutes, no device open — laptops closed or lids down. The hook worth landing: over 5,000 confirmed exoplanets, and almost none of them have ever been seen as a dot of light. Everything we claim to know about them came out of graphs like the one they are about to make. Question 1 asks for a sketch of brightness over time. Collect two or three on the board WITHOUT saying which is right. The two predictions worth having visible all lesson: "the star goes dark" and "the brightness does not come back up". Question 5 sends students back to their own sketch to compare. Question 4 asks how much a planet dims a star: 50%, 1%, or 0.0001%. Almost everyone guesses far too large. Do not correct it — Part B will.

Question 2 and its diagram. The geometry is the whole of question 31 later, moved to someone else's solar system, so it is worth the three minutes. Ask: "Stand up. If I am the Sun and Marcus is Earth, who in this room could ever transit?" Only someone physically between them. That is the entire rule. Question 3 extends it: Mercury orbits 2.6x as often as Venus, so it gets 2.6x as many chances to line up — seven Mercury transits against four of Venus between 2003 and 2049. Same reasoning turns up on question 32.

Five minutes, together, before anyone touches a slider. TWO THINGS TO POINT AT, and only two. (1) The two tick boxes are OFF. "Show measurement guides" marks WHERE to measure without giving values — that is the middle rung for a student who cannot find the baseline. "Reveal the values" prints the numbers and is for CHECKING, after they have measured. (2) The view control, above the graph. "Now showing" names the view you are in; the button beside it names the view it takes you to. Students get sent between the two views four times in this lab. This was rebuilt after the first run — the button used to carry the name of the CURRENT view, which read as an instruction to a student already looking at it. Also worth naming now: "Pin this curve" keeps the previous run on the graph as a dashed grey line. They will need it in ten minutes and will not find it under pressure.

Questions 5 to 7, about 20 minutes. This is reading practice and nothing else. Question 5 sends them back to their own sketch from question 1. The two things most students miss: the brightness returns to EXACTLY where it started, and the dip is far shallower and far more sharp-sided than they drew. Question 6: estimate depth and duration off the axes with "Show measurement guides" on, THEN tick "Reveal the values" and record both. Readings within about 15% are good work at this stage. Starting system reads 1.02% and 3.20 h. Question 7: the 30-day survey. Seven dips in the window; period 4.08 days. A student who counts 7 dips and divides 30 by 7 gets 4.3 — praise it, then point out the window does not start and end on a transit, so measuring one gap directly is better. Circulate with one question: "Which of the three is that number?"

Questions 8 to 16, about 25 minutes. The core of the lab. BE STRICT ABOUT ONE SLIDER. If two change, nothing can be concluded and the run has to be done again. This is the one place to insist — it is CCC2, cause and effect, and the rubric scores it. Run 1 (the reset state) is 1.02% / 3.20 h / 4.08 d. Run 2 gives 0.25% / 3.05 h / 4.08 d. Run 3 gives 0.25% / 6.14 h / 4.08 d. Run 4 gives 1.02% / 4.52 h / 11.55 d. Watch the clock: they need to be through Run 3 with fifteen minutes left, because question 11 is the slide after next and it is the reason the lab exists.

Question 9 — the r^2 relationship. Nathan's review: students at this level will not spot this unaided, so the packet builds it in four steps instead of asking for it. RUN IT IN THIS ORDER. Ask for the prediction FIRST, out loud, before anything on this slide: "You halved the planet. What happens to the depth?" Almost everyone says "half". Let them commit to it. Then (a) 1.02 / 0.25 = about 4. The division corrects them, not you. Then (b) name the fraction: one quarter. Then (c) this picture — count the small squares inside the big one. Four. Countable, not abstract. Only then (d), the sentence: the dip measures AREA, area depends on radius SQUARED, so the depth follows the square of the radius. The box underneath in the packet — "the squaring rule" — is the lookup they will use in Parts C, F and G. Point at it and tell them to fold the corner.

Leave this up while they finish question 9(d) and work question 10. This is a reference slide, not new content. The point of putting it on the board is that Part C is five minutes away and the square root will arrive with no warning otherwise. If a student asks why it is squared and not cubed: the planet blocks a flat disc of the star's face, and the area of a disc goes as r^2. Volume would be cubed. Do not go further than that — the relation is INTRODUCED as a ratio of areas here, not derived. That is the assessment boundary.

THIS IS THE SLIDE. Question 11 is the hinge of the whole lab and Nathan asked for it by name. Put this up only AFTER both runs are recorded in their tables. Do not show it early. Both curves on the same axes. Run 2 was a small planet on a normal star. Run 3 was a big planet on a giant star. Two systems with nothing in common. Give them thirty seconds of silence to look at it before you say anything. Ask for what they notice, not for the answer. What should come out: the depths are IDENTICAL — both land on the same green line at 0.25%. The durations are not: 3.0 hours against 6.1 hours, twice as long. Then the next slide asks the question that matters.

THE FIVE MINUTES THAT MATTER MOST. Questions 11 and 12. Ask the question on the slide and WAIT. Do not accept the first hand. Turn-and-talk for sixty seconds if the room is quiet, then take three answers. The answer is no. And the only things that tell the two systems apart are the DURATION, and knowing how big the star is. Then question 12 makes it concrete: an astronomer measures 0.25% and nothing else. Can they work out how big the planet is? No. They need the star's radius first — and that comes from somewhere else entirely, from the star's temperature and brightness. That is the H-R diagram, which they already have from Lesson 2. EVERYTHING AFTER THIS DEPENDS ON IT. Part C is the arithmetic of this. Question 33 is the state-exam version of it. The CER cannot be answered without it. Before you move on, get ONE SENTENCE from a student, in their words, and write it on the board. Leave it there for the rest of the lab. Day 2 opens by reading it back.

Questions 13 to 15. Faster than Run 3 — the surprise is behind them now. Question 14: why depth did not change. Depth is set by the two radii and nothing else. Moving the planet changes neither. Question 15 names Kepler's Third Law. They met it in the PhET Kepler's Laws lab the meeting before — this is the same law showing up in someone else's solar system, which is worth saying out loud. It comes back as the whole of Part D. Question 16 closes Part B: fill in depth / duration / period in three sentences. If they can do that from memory, Part B worked.

Point back at question 9 explicitly — question 17 asks them to do exactly that, and the answer is "because depth is a ratio of AREAS and area goes as radius squared". Where the star's radius comes from: the H-R Diagram in the reference tables, where the diagonal lines are marked in solar radii. They used that chart in Lesson 2. This is the lab's one genuine cross-lesson dependency. Do not derive the depth equation. Assessment boundary: it is INTRODUCED as a ratio of areas.

WORK THIS ON THE BOARD. Do not let them read it. These students are at Algebra I and the packet carries the same example in print so they can follow back. These numbers appear nowhere else in the lab, so nothing is given away. Make every student press the square-root key on their own calculator during step 2. Test value: root 0.25 should give 0.5. A student who cannot find the key now will stall silently for the next twenty minutes. STEP 1 IS THE WHOLE GAME. Forgetting to convert the percentage is the most common error on this page: root(1.00) = 1.00, which would make the planet exactly as wide as its star. The sanity check at the bottom is how they catch it themselves. Then questions 18 and 19 are the same three steps twice, and question 21 checks it against TRAPPIST-1e in the simulator.

This is question 11 again, now with numbers attached. If question 11 landed, this slide is a confirmation; if it did not, this is the second chance. Question 21 sends them to the TRAPPIST-1e preset in the simulator to check: a planet SMALLER than Earth (0.92 R⊕) making a 0.50% dip — a huge, easily detected signal — because the star is only 0.119 R☉ across. Compare with Kepler-452b on Day 2: nearly twice the size of TRAPPIST-1e, and a dip 28 times shallower. END OF DAY 1 HERE, after question 21.

Five minutes. Read back the sentence a student gave you yesterday — the one you wrote on the board at question 11. From a student, not from you. Then the three bullets, as a check: ask the room which measurement answers which question before you reveal them. KEPLER, worth sixty seconds: between 2009 and 2018 it stared at one patch of sky and measured the brightness of 150,000 stars at once, looking for exactly the dips they spent yesterday making. It found more than 2,600 planets. The data in Part D is that telescope's actual data. Hand out the NASA/JPL light-curve page now. Part D needs it; it does not need a device.

They met R^3 = T^2 in the PhET Kepler's Laws lab. The star-mass term is the only new thing on this slide; say so, so it does not read as a new law. The unit trap is the error that actually costs them. Days straight into the formula gives an answer in the tens of thousands of AU. The sanity check: "is that plausible for a planet that orbits in three days?" Next slide works an example before anyone starts question 22. Do not skip it.

JUPITER IS IN NEITHER TABLE. It models the method without answering a row of question 22 or 23. Sanity check worth saying: Jupiter really is about 5 times further out than Earth, and it really is about 778 million km. The method produces a number you can check against something you already know — that is the only reason to trust it on Kepler-6b. Two conversions they need all page: 1 year = 365 days, 1 AU = 149.6 million km.

25 minutes. Question 22 first (our own solar system, M_s = 1), then question 23 (the exoplanets). Say the tolerance out loud before they start. A student who thinks their 3.3 days is wrong because their neighbour got 3.2 will stop working. Question 24: the first four all orbit at about 0.05 AU — one eighth of Mercury's distance — and whip round in three to five DAYS. Kepler-452b is at 1.05 AU and takes 385 days. Question 25 is the one worth discussing as a class: why were the hot Jupiters found first? Because confirming a planet needs several repeat dips. A 3-day period gives you a fresh one every three days; Kepler-452b transits once every 385 days, so confirming it took YEARS. They were not more common — just quicker to prove. Question 26: model says 0.668% for Kepler-5b, measurement says 0.725%. Not a broken model — real stars are dimmer at the edge and real transits cut off-centre. A 0.06% disagreement is EVIDENCE THE MODEL IS GOOD. Say that explicitly; it is the honest-science moment of Day 2.

20 minutes for Part E. The rule is GIVEN, not derived. Work the L = 0.25 example on the board. The only thing to emphasise: you take the square root ONCE and use it twice. Students routinely do root(0.95 x L). Then question 27 checks the rule on our own solar system first: zone runs 0.95 to 1.37 AU. Venus at 0.72 is too close — and Venus really is a runaway greenhouse at 465 C. Mars at 1.52 is outside — and Mars really has lost its surface water. THE RULE PASSES A TEST ON A SYSTEM WE CAN CHECK, which is the only reason to trust it on one we cannot. Say that sentence.

DO NOT MARK THIS AS A CLEAN "INSIDE" OR "OUTSIDE". This is deliberate and it is in the key. Full credit for recognising the planet is ON THE BOUNDARY. Full credit too for a student who picks a side but says the margin is too small to be sure. This is a real unresolved question about a real planet, not a trick, and it is a better science lesson than a clean answer would be. If a student is frustrated by that, that frustration IS the lesson — real measurements come with real uncertainty, and a scientist's job includes saying "the data cannot settle this". The CER in Part G is deliberately unambiguous by contrast, so they get one of each.

Question 30 is the conceptual payoff of Part E, and it is a classmate-says-X question, which is the format the state exam uses. A planet can orbit twelve times closer to its star than Mercury does to the Sun and still be temperate, provided the star is faint enough. The misconception being killed: comparing distances ACROSS two systems where the stars are nothing alike. It is the same error as reading a depth without knowing the star — the third time this lab has made the same point, now about luminosity instead of radius. Worth naming: that is the pattern of the whole lab. Every single measurement means nothing until you bring the star into it.

Question 33: same planet, two different stars, same star mass so the same orbital speed. Star A gives about 11 hours, Star B about 6. Accept +/- 1 hour. 33(c) wants BOTH pieces of evidence and the rubric scores it: DURATION (same speed, so twice as long to cross means twice as far to go — A is wider) and DEPTH (A's dip is 0.45%, B's is 2.1%, so the same planet covers a much smaller fraction of A). Star A is 1.50 R-sun and Star B is 0.70 — a ratio of 2.1, which matches both. Watch for students reading the two graphs against each other: THE TWO BRIGHTNESS SCALES ARE DIFFERENT. It says so under the figure and they will miss it.

DO THIS TOGETHER, on the board, before anybody calculates anything. It is the single highest-value five minutes in Part G. The arithmetic is three calculator steps and is not where the credit lives. The hard part — the part Nathan's review identified — is deciding WHICH NUMBER FEEDS WHICH EQUATION. Answers: depth and star's RADIUS -> Step 1, the size. Period and star's MASS -> Step 2, the distance. Luminosity -> Step 3, the zone. 90 days is NOT NEEDED — it only tells you about three dips were seen. The unneeded number is deliberate. Students at this level assume every number given must be used, and the state exam does not work that way. Watch for the two 0.30s being swapped. Radius sizes the planet; mass places the orbit. They are not interchangeable and the answer looks plausible either way.

DEBRIEF SLIDE. Do not show it until they have worked the frame in the packet, or you have given them the answers. If time is short: do the data organiser together in class and set the three steps plus the claim and reasoning as homework. The organiser is the part that needs you in the room. WHAT SEPARATES PROFICIENT WORK, and it is not the arithmetic: whether the student brings the STAR into every single step — its radius to size the planet, its mass to place the orbit, its luminosity to judge the temperature. A student who reports "0.077 AU, very close, so too hot" has done every calculation correctly and still missed the lesson of the whole lab. Unlike question 28, this one has a clean answer on purpose. The planet sits about a third of the way across the zone, comfortably inside, no boundary ambiguity. FOR AN EARLY FINISHER: what single measurement would settle whether this planet actually has water? None of these — you would need a spectrum of its atmosphere taken during transit, which is what JWST now does.

The sentence frames are new in the rebuild. They are in the packet too. Students at this level can do the calculation and then write three sentences that just restate the answers; the frames force the word "because". Say the green box out loud. It is the assessment criterion stated as a sentence, and students write better when they know what is being scored. A proficient Reasoning paragraph hits all three: the depth needed the star's radius, the period needed the star's mass, the distance needed the star's luminosity. Three "because"s.
