WHAT THIS DECK IS: the projection companion to the guided-notes packet "Guided Notes: Planets and Orbits" (ess-u1-guided-notes-orbits.pdf). Slides follow the packet Part for Part. Students fill in their blanks as each slide comes up. NOT the teaching deck. The 5E instruction lives in U1_Discovering_New_Worlds_L3_Slides. This is end-of-unit consolidation: content knowledge only. EVERY BLANK ANSWER is shown in gold. Advance, let students write, then discuss. STANDARDS: HS-ESS1-4 use mathematical or computational representations to predict the motion of orbiting objects · ESS1.B(1) Kepler's laws describe common features of the motions of orbiting objects · CCC1(4) mathematical representations are needed to identify some patterns · CCC3(5) algebraic thinking predicts the effect of a change in one variable on another. ASSESSMENT BOUNDARY: never more than two bodies, never calculus. Gravity as the CAUSE belongs to Unit 2 — if students ask why the relationship holds, that is the honest answer. TIMING: about one class period at pace, two if you work the calculation questions.

PACKET: Part A, the table and the key term. THE COMMON MISCONCEPTION: that different planets have different KINDS of water. They do not. Same molecule, different energy.

PACKET: Part A, five blanks. PACKET QUESTION 1 — "a planet orbits at 1.35 AU, is it in the habitable zone?" The answer is YOU CANNOT TELL. In our system 1.35 AU is comfortable; HD-20782 b orbits at 1.352 AU and is far too close, because its star's zone starts at 2.613 AU. MAKE STUDENTS SAY IT: "habitable" is never a distance on its own. It is a distance relative to THAT star. This is the single most common error on this content.

PACKET: Part B, the table and three blanks. THIS TABLE IS THE WHOLE ARGUMENT IN FOUR ROWS. Have students compare the min and max columns before you reveal anything — the contrast between the first three rows and Borrelly does the teaching. WATCH FOR students who read Borrelly's 3.590 AU average and conclude it is "between Mars and Europa, so it should be ice." That is exactly the trap the average sets.

PACKET: Part B, the infobox and question 2. PACKET QUESTION 2 — two planets, same average distance from the same star, one circular and one stretched. The circular one is the better candidate: same average, completely different experience. THIS IS THE SENTENCE TO MAKE THEM WRITE: an average hides variation. They will need it again in Part F.

PACKET: Part C, three blanks. WE NEVER SEE THE PLANET. We see the star get slightly dimmer. Students find this genuinely surprising and it is worth a beat. THE DIP IS TINY — about 0.35% for Kepler-5b. Hold up a hand in front of a lamp across the room to make the scale real.

PACKET: Part C figure. HOW TO READ IT: find any two consecutive dips and measure the gap between them. That gap IS the orbital period. THE DEPTH IS THE SAME EVERY TIME because the same planet blocks the same fraction of light. If a second planet were present you would see dips of a DIFFERENT depth interleaved. SOURCE: NASA JPL, "Exploring Exoplanets with Kepler."

PACKET: Part D, first blank. THE CURVE BENDS UPWARD. That is what "increases faster than the distance does" looks like, and it is why a straight-line intuition fails here. EVERY POINT SITS ON THE CURVE — including Comet Borrelly, at its AVERAGE distance. Kepler's Third Law does not care about orbit shape; it only ever gives you the average. That limitation is Part E's reason to exist.

PACKET: Part D, second blank. THE HONEST POINT: the graph is not WRONG. It is not PRECISE. The equation gives 13.57 AU, which sits inside the class's range — but a range that wide cannot support a confident claim.

PACKET: Part D, four blanks. THE SIMPLIFICATION MATTERS: the equation students derived themselves in Explore 2 WAS Kepler's Third Law — it just had M = 1 hidden inside it, because every object in that data set orbited the same Sun. Their equation was right. It was a special case. UNITS ARE NOT OPTIONAL HERE. The law only takes this clean form in AU, Earth years and solar masses. In SI units it carries a gravitational constant. Students do not need that, but they do need to use the right units.

PACKET: Part D, question 3 worked in full. THE TWO ERRORS TO HUNT FOR: forgetting M entirely, and taking the cube root before multiplying. ON A CALCULATOR: cube root is x^(1/3). Many students will try to use the square-root key. Show it once. PACKET QUESTION 4: two planets, same period, one star nine times heavier — the heavier star's planet orbits further out, by the cube root of 9 = 2.08 times. Counterintuitive until you read the equation, which is the point.

PACKET: Part E, Term 1. A IS NOT A NEW QUANTITY. It is the number they already calculated for every exoplanet. Say so explicitly — students treat the two laws as unrelated unless you connect them here. ECCENTRICITY IS EXAGGERATED in this drawing. A real planetary orbit is far rounder. Say it, or they will leave thinking orbits look like this.

PACKET: Part E, Term 2, two blanks. THE SUN IS NOT QUITE AT THE CENTRE — it is at a FOCUS. The two are the same point only for a perfect circle. This is a classic exam distinction. FOR THE CONSTRUCTION: habitable zone distances are always measured from the focus holding the STAR, never from the centre of the ellipse.

PACKET: Part E, three blanks. ECCENTRICITY HAS NO UNITS — it is a distance divided by a distance. Students lose marks writing "0.0167 AU". PACKET QUESTION 5: TOI-2257 b, e = 0.496 on a 25 cm major axis, so f = 12.4 cm — half the width of the whole orbit. Compare Earth: f = 1.0 cm on a 60 cm orbit, which is visually nothing. PACKET QUESTION 6, THE CHAIN TO MEMORISE: low e → small f → foci almost on top of each other → nearly circular → same distance all the way round → constant energy → stable temperature.

PACKET: Part F, the summary table. IF STUDENTS REMEMBER ONE SLIDE FROM THIS LESSON, make it this one. The laws are tools with jobs, not formulas to memorise.

PACKET: Part F table. TWO ROWS DO ALL THE TEACHING: HD-20782 b orbits FURTHEST of all eight (1.352 AU) and is still too close — because its star's zone starts at 2.613 AU. Habitable is relative to the star. TOI-2257 b is in the zone on average and still fails, because e = 0.496 stretches its orbit right out of the zone for a large part of every revolution. It is Comet Borrelly again. TRAPPIST-1 g ONLY JUST MAKES IT: 0.047 against an outer limit of 0.048. Good place to ask how confident we should be in a claim that close to the boundary.

PACKET: Part F, questions 7 and 8. THESE TWO ANSWERS ARE THE MOST LIKELY SHORT-RESPONSE ITEMS on an assessment of this lesson. Make students write them in full sentences, not fragments. CCC 3 — SCALE, PROPORTION AND QUANTITY: algebraic thinking used to examine scientific data and predict the effect of a change in one variable on another.

PACKET: Part G, four blanks. THIS IS THE PERFORMANCE TASK ARGUMENT. Every student's revised model should contain these four links in this order. If a model has distance but not shape, it is incomplete — that is the single most common gap. PACKET QUESTION 9 — the journalist who writes that a planet "has liquid water": TWO things are wrong. (1) Without eccentricity we do not know whether it STAYS in the zone. (2) The habitable zone is about POSSIBILITY, not fact — our technology can only detect water inside our own solar system.

PACKET: the closing vocabulary box. THE TWO LAWS ARE WORTH MEMORISING IN BOTH FORMS — the equation and the sentence. Students who only memorise the symbols cannot tell you what they are for. GRAVITY IS NOT ON THIS SHEET. If students ask WHY these relationships hold, the honest answer is that it is gravity, and that it is Unit 2. The assessment boundary for HS-ESS1-4 explicitly keeps gravitational attraction and calculus out of this lesson.
